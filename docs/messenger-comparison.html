⁴ per-msg (DR+sym) — common baseline everywhere: SenderKeys, a shared session key that advances via a symmetric ratchet (a one-directional HMAC-SHA256 chain) with every message — per-message forward secrecy. In groups (N>2) that's the whole story: as with the Double Ratchet, recovery from a compromise requires an explicit sender-key rotation, no post-compromise security (PCS). In rooms with exactly two people, where a pairwise DH ratchet is tractable (unlike groups, where fan-out prevents it), a DH ratchet is folded into the same state — reconstructing on top of SenderKeys the two classic Double Ratchet components, not a separate subsystem, no new frame on the wire. Under normal conditions whoever steals the state reads exactly one more message beyond the ones already intercepted: from the next one on, the chain is secure again (post-compromise security).

¹⁸ Briar — direct P2P over Bluetooth/WiFi, not a mesh — besides Tor, Briar can sync messages over local Bluetooth or WiFi with no internet, but only single-hop: between two devices that are already mutual contacts and physically in range (roughly 10-30 meters) at the same time. No automatic relay through intermediate devices toward contacts never met in person — "multi-hop via social mesh" is research the project itself states is not yet implemented (GitLab issue #1816). It also requires an in-person QR exchange beforehand to become contacts: there's no discovery of nearby strangers. In practice: useful only within already-connected, physically nearby circles, not a network that spreads through a crowd of strangers who simply installed the app. Worth noting too: at the 10-30 meter range Bluetooth/WiFi actually needs, two people are almost always close enough to just talk or signal to each other directly — the real use case isn't "generic blackout resilience" but the much narrower "silent/discreet communication when you can't afford to speak, while still standing right next to each other".

¹⁹ bitchat — real multi-hop mesh, unlike Briar (note ¹⁸) — automatic relay through intermediate devices with an initial TTL of 7, source-routed when a bidirectionally-confirmed path exists, otherwise controlled flooding with LRU deduplication (1000 packets, 5 minutes). Bluetooth LE range remains 10-30 meters per single hop, but the relay chain extends effective reach beyond that, without requiring the two endpoints to already be mutual contacts for public chat (DMs, on the other hand, still require an established Noise session). Source: the project's official WHITEPAPER.md.

²⁰ bitchat — three distinct protocols, forward secrecy on only one of them — Noise XX (Curve25519 + ChaCha20-Poly1305 + SHA-256) for live sessions inside the Bluetooth mesh, with mutual authentication and forward secrecy; Noise X (one-way) for "sealed" mail delivered offline by intermediate couriers, authenticated but without forward secrecy — compromising the recipient's static key exposes mail not yet delivered; XChaCha20-Poly1305 with HKDF-SHA256 from secp256k1 ECDH for the Nostr-relay bridge (internet path between mutual favorites), also without forward secrecy. Source: the project's official WHITEPAPER.md.

²¹ bitchat — partial sealed sender — couriers relaying offline sealed mail learn neither sender, recipient, nor content (opaque addressing via an HMAC of the UTC day). On the live Bluetooth mesh, however, the 8-byte sender ID travels in the clear in every packet header and never rotates: a passive listener can enumerate participants and track their movements over time — a weakness the whitepaper itself explicitly acknowledges.

²² bitchat — partial zero persistence — no server of its own (P2P mesh + third-party Nostr relays), but every mesh node keeps a local cache of the public history (gossip sync) for up to 6 hours, persisted to disk; the external Nostr relays used for the internet bridge may retain the encrypted envelopes under their own policy, outside bitchat's control.

²³ bitchat — partial E2E groups — default public channels are Ed25519-signed broadcasts but sent in the clear, readable by anyone within mesh range. Only optional password-protected channels are encrypted, with a shared symmetric key derived via Argon2id and then used with AES-256-GCM — known to every channel member, not an individual per-member sender key.

²⁴ bitchat — partial message signing — only public announcements and broadcast messages on the mesh are Ed25519-signed (the TTL byte is excluded from the signature, so relays can decrement it without invalidating it). Private messages (DMs) are authenticated by the Noise session's AEAD, not by a separate verifiable signature.

Sources: GitHub permissionlesstech/bitchat issue #376; Trail of Bits, Building secure messaging is hard (July 18, 2025).

³⁷ Neutrino — hybrid post-quantum — hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) scheme for the WebSocket handshake, sender_key delivery, and knocks; the identity keys (transport key and the relationship key with each contact) additionally carry parallel ML-DSA-65 (FIPS 204) signatures on key rotation and remote self-destruction, plus an optional per-room ML-DSA-65 signature on chat messages.

Discord — no E2E: all messages are readable in plaintext by Discord Inc. Permanent server-side logs. Cooperation with authorities is standard.

WeChat — a tool of Chinese state surveillance: Tencent is legally required to provide the Chinese government access to all communications. All messages are archived, indexed and available. Living in China without WeChat is practically impossible.

Telegram — regular chats and all groups/channels are NOT E2E (Telegram holds the keys). Only "secret chats" (manually started, mobile only, 1:1 only) are E2E with partial forward secrecy. Backup, migration and multi-device sync

What identity consists of

A user's identity is made up of three pieces: the Ed25519 identity key (defines the .onion address on Tor or the .b32.i2p address on I2P, depending on the network chosen at startup — the only unrecoverable piece of data), the durable post-quantum keys (ML-KEM-768 for key exchange, ML-DSA-65 for signatures — generated independently, not derived from the 24 words), and the contact list (name, address and keys for each person, including the keys dedicated to each relationship — one authenticates the connection, one signs and encrypts what's exchanged with them — generated automatically when they're added).

PC → phone migration

Migration happens via an encrypted archive: the PC exports everything into an encrypted file or a sequence of QR codes; the phone imports it — under Termux from the file or the .chunk parts saved next to each QR, in the native Android app (in testing) also by scanning the QR codes. Offline channel, no server.

Contact export — encrypted file or QR

The neutrino sync export command exports the contact list in encrypted form, choosing between two channels:

  • Encrypted file — a single binary file (NTRINO format) transferable via USB, cloud or any medium. The encryption key is derived from the identity seed (HKDF), never transmitted.
  • QR code — the address book is automatically split into chunks and shown as a sequence of QR codes, printable or scannable offline, without writing anything to disk.

The neutrino sync import <file|chunk…> command accepts a file or one or more QR chunks and merges the contacts non-destructively: union by identity key, no contact ever deleted. Chunks can be imported in separate sessions.

The entire transfer happens offline — no server involved, no plaintext data on disk.

Multi-device sync (hybrid)

Hybrid approach when the two contact lists diverge:

  • Automatic, same local network — /sec-indoor-sync: two devices holding the SAME identity on the same Wi-Fi/LAN find each other on their own (UDP multicast, no Tor/I2P needed — they already share the identity key, so no key exchange is required) and merge their address books. Union per contact (by identity key), never a deletion: an existing entry only takes the other side's non-empty local name or updated ML-KEM key.
  • Manual, any time — neutrino sync export/sync import: when the devices aren't on the same network, one exports the address book as an encrypted file or QR code, the other imports and merges it.

Identity backup — two-tier scheme

Level 1 — 24 words (BIP-39). The hidden service's Ed25519 seed (32 bytes) is encoded as 24 mnemonic words. The user writes them down on paper on first launch. Restoring with only the 24 words → same .onion, empty contacts, post-quantum keys regenerated from scratch (independent of the seed, not recoverable from the words alone).

Level 2 — encrypted file. Full backup (contacts, saved groups, post-quantum keys) encrypted with a key derived from the 24 words themselves (HKDF). Stored on USB or cloud. A full restore creates an additional device of the same identity: it gets its own network address, and the relationship keys with each contact are negotiated again, never copied.

One secret to remember, two levels of recovery — the same model as modern hardware wallets.

Decoy identity password (duress)

Besides the real password, every keystore has a mandatory decoy identity with its own password. Entered at unlock time, it opens the decoy — an ordinary-looking identity — and at the same instant overwrites every other identity in the file with random bytes. Designed for a coercion scenario: whoever forces you to unlock the device sees a normal login and has no way of knowing that a real identity existed and has just been destroyed. On the Android app in testing the same password wipes identity and address book instead, indistinguishable from a plain wrong password.

Glossary — terms and acronyms

Security concepts

E2E — End-to-End (encryption)
Encryption happens on the sender's device and is decrypted only on the reader's device. No server, company or intermediary can read the content.
Forward secrecy
If someone obtains today's cryptographic keys, they cannot decrypt past conversations, because every session or message uses different keys that are destroyed after use.
• per-session — the keys change on every new connection
• per-message — every single message has its own unique key
Sealed sender
The server that routes messages doesn't know the identity of who wrote them: the "who sent this" information is encrypted inside the payload and visible only to the recipient.
Zero persistence
The server doesn't save any message to disk. If the process restarts, everything disappears.
Safety numbers
Alphanumeric codes derived from the users' cryptographic keys. If two people compare them outside the platform and they match, they know with certainty they're talking to each other.
• In most messengers: manual comparison
• In Neutrino: manual comparison like elsewhere, but with a mandatory out-of-band contact method required by /grp-contact-send — you can't send a contact card without stating a way to verify it outside the app
Message signing
Every message carries a digital signature that proves who wrote it and that the content wasn't altered in transit.

Cryptographic protocols

Double Ratchet
Protocol developed by Signal. Each message uses a different key generated by a "chain" that advances irreversibly. Periodically the two parties exchange new ephemeral public keys. Used by Signal, WhatsApp.
SenderKeys
Protocol for group messaging: each member distributes a symmetric key that advances via a symmetric ratchet. More efficient than Double Ratchet for large groups. Used by Signal (groups) and Neutrino (rooms).
X3DH — Extended Triple Diffie-Hellman
Initial key agreement used by Signal and WhatsApp to establish a shared secret even if one of the users is offline. Precedes Double Ratchet in the startup sequence.
NOISE Protocol
Framework for secure communication protocols, designed for simplicity and formal verifiability. Used by Briar, Keet, Ricochet Refresh and Cwtch.
MTProto 2.0
Telegram's proprietary protocol. Used in E2E mode only in 1:1 "secret chats". All group chats and channels are not E2E.
Megolm + Olm
Matrix/Element's cryptographic protocols. Olm = Double Ratchet for direct messages; Megolm = a group protocol similar to SenderKeys but with per-session-only forward secrecy.
AES-256-GCM
Industry-standard symmetric encryption algorithm. Used by all modern messengers for the actual message content.
ECIES
Elliptic-curve asymmetric encryption scheme. Used in Neutrino to distribute session keys to peers.
ML-KEM — Module-Lattice Key Encapsulation Mechanism
Post-quantum key exchange mechanism standardized by NIST (FIPS 203, known as Kyber during standardization). Used in hybrid form, never alone, alongside a classical elliptic curve: Neutrino (ML-KEM-768) and Signal (Kyber-1024, pre-standardization name).
ML-DSA — Module-Lattice Digital Signature Algorithm
Post-quantum digital signature scheme standardized by NIST (FIPS 204, known as Dilithium during standardization). Used by Neutrino (ML-DSA-65) alongside its existing Ed25519 signatures on the network identity.
MLS — Messaging Layer Security (RFC 9420)
IETF-standardized protocol for group messaging, designed for forward secrecy and post-compromise security that stay efficient even in very large groups. Used by Wire; Matrix aims to converge on it; Discord uses it in classic form (no post-quantum components) for audio/video calls via the DAVE protocol.

Network architectures

P2P — Peer-to-Peer
There is no central server: participants connect directly to each other. Whoever hosts the room acts as a relay, but only during the session.
Centralized
All messages pass through the servers of the company running the service. The company has access to metadata and, if encryption is absent or in its hands, to content as well.
Federated
Like email: many independent servers communicate with each other. A user registers on one but can message someone on another. No single company controls the entire network.
DHT — Distributed Hash Table
Distributed system for finding other users without a central server: each node holds a small portion of the global index. Used by Session (Oxen), Keet (Hyperswarm), Tox, Jami (OpenDHT).
SMP relay (SimpleX)
Messages pass through relay servers that only receive encrypted blobs without knowing who the sender or recipient is. No persistent identity.
Tor — The Onion Router
Anonymization network: traffic passes through at least three intermediate nodes, each of which only knows the adjacent node. Makes it very difficult to associate a connection with a physical IP.
Hidden service / .onion address
A service reachable only via Tor. The address is mathematically derived from the server's public key. It has no fixed IP, and the two ends of the connection don't know each other's IP addresses.
I2P — Invisible Internet Project
Anonymization network alternative to Tor, using unidirectional tunnels — separate ones for inbound and outbound traffic — instead of Tor's bidirectional circuit, plus "garlic" routing (multiple encrypted messages bundled into one packet). A service reachable on I2P has a .b32.i2p address, conceptually analogous to Tor's .onion hidden service. Used by Neutrino as an alternative network to Tor.

Identity and keys

Public key / private key
A mathematically linked pair: the public key is shared and identifies the user; the private key stays secret and is used to sign and decrypt.
Ed25519
Elliptic-curve digital signature algorithm. Fast, compact keys (32 bytes). Used in Neutrino for contact identity and to derive the network address (.onion on Tor, .b32.i2p on I2P).
Relationship keys
Randomly generated key pairs, independent for each contact — never shared, never global. One authenticates the connection with that specific contact, the other signs and encrypts the messages and files exchanged with them. Exchanged automatically as soon as two users add each other to their address book.
BIP-39 / mnemonic seed
Standard that encodes 32 bytes of secret as 24 common words. Universal standard for backing up cryptographic keys. In Neutrino, the 24 words encode the Ed25519 seed of the identity (Tor hidden service or I2P destination, depending on the network chosen at startup).
Autocrypt / OpenPGP
E2E encryption standard for email. Used by Delta Chat as a transparent encryption layer over regular email providers. No forward secrecy.
